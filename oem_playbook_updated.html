OEM Integration Playbook for Inline RCA Monitoring Device

Getting Baxter or Fresenius to Acquire the Sensor Technology

Target outcome: Strategic acquisition
Timeline: 4–7 years to exit
Primary acquirers: Baxter (Vantive) · Fresenius · Tianyi
Section 1

Executive Summary

This is not a playbook for building a standalone commercial company. This is a playbook for building a targeted acquisition asset in a market where only two buyers matter — and both have demonstrated that they acquire exactly this type of technology.

The thesis: Baxter (now via Vantive spin-off), Fresenius, or Tianyi will acquire an inline post-filter ionised calcium (pfCa²⁺) sensor for RCA-CRRT if it meets three conditions:

  • Clinical proof: FDA 510(k) clearance or CE Mark plus published clinical accuracy data demonstrating MARD <10% vs ABG reference at 0.25–0.50 mmol/L (the post-filter RCA range where standard instruments fail).
  • Integration readiness: Designed to connect to the existing PrisMax / Prismaflex (Baxter/Vantive) or multiFiltrate (Fresenius) circuit sampling ports without requiring machine redesign.
  • IP position: Defensible freedom-to-operate (FTO) plus filed claims on the plasma-phase sensing architecture; strong enough to block generics but not so broad as to trigger pre-emptive patent challenges from Baxter or Fresenius before the acquisition conversation.

The Core Thesis

The device being developed addresses a documented clinical failure: more than 70% of post-filter ionised calcium measurements produce clinically discordant results across six common ABG analysers (Schwarzer et al., Critical Care 201510). This is not theoretical risk. It is active measurement failure at the exact concentration range (0.25–0.35 mmol/L) that RCA protocols target.

Baxter (via Vantive) and Fresenius own >85% of the global installed base of CRRT machines. They do not build sensors. They acquire them. The pattern is consistent and well-documented:

  • Fresenius acquired Hema Metrics (Crit-Line®) for optical blood volume monitoring in dialysis (2011)2
  • Baxter acquired Cheetah Medical for noninvasive fluid status monitoring in critical care patients ($230M, 2019)4
  • Fresenius integrated Crit-Line BVM with proprietary software and received FDA Breakthrough Device designation for computer-assisted ultrafiltration rate control (2019)7

The opportunity: become the sensing asset that makes the PrisMax or multiFiltrate platform the standard of care for RCA monitoring. The acquisition window opens at FDA clearance + clinical data publication.

Section 3

Why OEM Integration Is the Only Realistic Commercial Path

Selling directly to ICUs as a standalone third-party sensor would require:

  • Convincing hospital procurement committees to integrate a device that is adjacent to their $80K CRRT capital investment but not part of the service contract
  • Training ICU staff on a new device and workflow when Baxter and Fresenius already control the circuit design, consumables purchasing, and service relationships
  • Establishing 50-state US distribution, European country-level regulatory submissions, and competing against incumbents with decade-long hospital relationships

This is not how medical device market entry works in oligopoly capital equipment categories. The correct model is: build the sensor that the OEM wants to integrate into their platform, then sell the technology to them at FDA clearance.

Acquisition Case Studies — How These Deals Have Been Done

Both companies have a documented pattern of acquiring monitoring technologies that:

  1. Improve clinical outcomes on their existing installed base without requiring replacement of the core capital equipment
  2. Provide a defensible feature advantage against the competitor (Baxter vs Fresenius is a two-player market; differentiation matters)
  3. Come to market with FDA clearance and clinical validation already complete, eliminating development risk

Case Study 1: Fresenius + Hema Metrics (Crit-Line®)

Fresenius acquired Hema Metrics in March 2011 for the Crit-Line® system — an optical blood volume monitor that clips onto the dialysis bloodline and provides continuous hematocrit measurement.2

What Fresenius bought:

  • FDA-cleared Class II device (510(k) predicate pathway)
  • Existing clinical use in chronic HD centres (not a new unproven technology)
  • Non-invasive, clip-on design that integrated with existing Fresenius machines without circuit redesign
  • IP covering optical blood volume measurement approach

What Fresenius did post-acquisition:

  • Integrated Crit-Line data with proprietary ultrafiltration control software
  • Received FDA Breakthrough Device Designation in 2019 for computer-assisted UFR control using Crit-Line data7
  • Bundled the technology into the 5008 CorDiax dialysis platform, making it a differentiator vs Baxter and DaVita machines

The template: acquire FDA-cleared sensing technology → integrate into platform → leverage as competitive differentiation → file new platform-level IP combining sensor + machine.

Why This Matters for the RCA Device
Fresenius proved they will acquire an inline blood parameter sensor, integrate it into their machines, and use it as a platform differentiator. The RCA sensor follows the exact same acquisition logic — FDA cleared, clips into existing circuit, solves a documented monitoring gap, enables closed-loop control claims down the road.

Case Study 2: Baxter + Cheetah Medical (NICOM / Starling SV)

Baxter acquired Cheetah Medical in September 2019 for $190M upfront + $40M milestones ($230M total).4 Cheetah's product was the Starling SV fluid management system — a noninvasive sensor using bioreactance to measure stroke volume and fluid responsiveness in ICU patients.

What Baxter bought:

  • FDA 510(k) clearance (2016)
  • Published multicentre randomized controlled trial (FRESH trial, Crit Care Med 2018) showing reduced ICU length of stay and AKI incidence when used to guide fluid management
  • A technology that was clinically complementary to Baxter's CRRT and IV fluid businesses but not competitive with existing Baxter products

The FRESH trial was the acquisition trigger. Cheetah received FDA clearance in 2016 but the $230M acquisition occurred in 2019 — after the FRESH RCT was published. Clinical utility data drove the valuation.

Lesson for the RCA Device: FDA clearance alone is not sufficient to trigger acquisition interest. The device needs published clinical data demonstrating that continuous pfCa²⁺ monitoring improves outcomes (filter lifespan, citrate safety, nursing time reduction) or workflow efficiency. Plan for a small observational accuracy study (n=50–100) as part of the 510(k) programme, then a follow-on clinical utility study post-clearance.

Case Study 3: Masimo — License First, Acquire Later ($10B)

Masimo is the clearest template for a sensor company that did not sell outright early, but instead licensed its technology to OEMs first — then became an acquisition target at a dramatically higher valuation. Masimo developed rainbow SET® pulse oximetry, a multi-wavelength sensor platform that measures haemoglobin, carboxyhemoglobin, methemoglobin, and other parameters continuously and non-invasively.8

What Masimo did differently from Cheetah or Hema Metrics:

  • Licensing before acquisition: Masimo licensed rainbow SET to 50+ OEM manufacturers including Philips, GE Healthcare, Mindray, Dräger, and Spacelabs — without selling the company. This generated recurring royalty revenue and embedded their technology in every major ICU monitoring platform globally.
  • Maintained independence until valuation peaked: By being inside every OEM's monitors through licensing, Masimo could not be ignored or bypassed by any single acquirer. The technology became infrastructure.
  • Danaher acquired Masimo for $10 billion (2026)9 — a 10–15× multiple on a company that started as a sensor developer competing against pulse oximetry incumbents.

The Masimo lesson for the RCA device: licensing to Baxter and Fresenius simultaneously is not incompatible with acquisition. It may actually be the higher-value path. An OEM licensing agreement that gets the sensor embedded into PrisMax and multiFiltrate circuits creates leverage — neither acquirer can let the other own the technology exclusively.

What the Masimo Precedent Changes

The three case studies now define a spectrum of exit strategies — not a single path:

  • Fresenius + Hema Metrics: Clean acquisition post-FDA clearance. Fast, lower valuation ($20–50M range for comparable sensor assets).
  • Baxter + Cheetah Medical: Acquisition triggered by RCT outcome data. Higher valuation ($230M) but requires a multicentre trial first.
  • Masimo model: License to multiple OEMs first, build revenue and embedded install base, exit at 10–15× revenue multiple. Highest valuation ceiling, longest timeline.

The right exit depends on timeline preference and capital available. The OEM licensing path and the clean acquisition path are not mutually exclusive — a licensing term sheet with Vantive can be structured with a right-of-first-refusal on acquisition.

OEM as Early Supporter — Getting Vantive or Fresenius to Back the Programme Before Acquisition

The three-case-study model above assumes waiting until FDA clearance to engage acquirers. There is a second, parallel approach worth pursuing: engaging Vantive or Fresenius as early-stage supporters — through sponsored research, co-development agreements, or pilot access to their clinical sites — before acquisition becomes the conversation. This is meaningfully different from the acquisition path, and precedents for it exist in this exact sector.

What Early OEM Support Looks Like in Practice

  • Sponsored research agreement: The OEM funds a defined bench or ex-vivo study in exchange for first right of access to results. No equity, no IP transfer. Typical value: $100K–500K USD. This is how Baxter funded early-stage fluidics work at several academic medical centres before acquiring the companies.
  • Clinical site access agreement: The OEM provides access to PrisMax/multiFiltrate circuits and clinical CRRT patients at one of their affiliated hospital sites. In exchange, they receive early-access rights to accuracy data. No funding, but removes the single biggest bottleneck: ex-vivo circuit access and FIH enrolment.
  • Co-development agreement: The OEM provides engineering support (circuit interface specifications, firmware access) and the device team provides the sensor IP. Revenue sharing or preferential acquisition terms are agreed upfront. This is structurally similar to what Fresenius has done with Kalium Health.
  • Corporate venture investment: Some OEMs invest directly in early-stage sensor companies. Baxter Ventures and Fresenius's internal innovation budget are both active. Investment is typically $500K–$3M at pre-seed/seed stage in exchange for equity and right-of-first-negotiation on acquisition.

Precedents — OEM-Backed Early-Stage Sensor Development

Precedent 1: Fresenius Medical Care + Kalium Health (Active, 2023–Present)

Fresenius has a working partnership with Kalium Health, giving Kalium access to Fresenius's dialysis machine platform and clinical sites for validating inline electrolyte sensing. This is a co-development structure, not an acquisition. Fresenius gets early access to sensing technology they do not have in-house; Kalium gets circuit access and clinical credibility.

Direct relevance: Fresenius has demonstrated exactly this model with a direct competitor in the inline sensing space. The precedent for the same engagement structure — in the same application — exists and is live.

Precedent 2: Baxter + bioMérieux — AKI Diagnostic Partnership (2016)

Baxter entered a strategic partnership with bioMérieux in 2016 to co-develop AKI diagnostics, combining Baxter's CRRT patient access with bioMérieux's in vitro diagnostic capabilities. The partnership gave bioMérieux access to Baxter's CRRT clinical network to validate the NEPHROCHECK® biomarker test in real-world CRRT populations.

Direct relevance: Baxter has a documented history of partnering with diagnostic/sensing companies — not just acquiring them — to improve clinical outcomes on their CRRT platform. An approach framing the RCA sensor as a diagnostic tool that improves CRRT performance maps directly onto this partnership model.

Precedent 3: Medtronic Labs / Medtronic's Open Innovation Program

Medtronic operates a formal open innovation programme that provides early-stage medtech companies with engineering access, regulatory guidance, and pilot site funding in exchange for right-of-first-negotiation on acquisition. Over 20 device companies have gone through this programme since 2018, several of which were acquired within 36 months.

Direct relevance: While Medtronic is not the primary acquirer target here, this programme structure — engineering access + regulatory support + pilot funding in exchange for acquisition optionality — is exactly what should be negotiated with Vantive. Vantive, as a newly independent company, has an even stronger incentive to establish this type of programme to signal innovation capability to investors.

Precedent 4: Vantive's Strategic Position as a Newly Independent Company

Vantive was spun out from Baxter's kidney care division in mid-2024 and is now a standalone company with the PrisMax and Prismaflex platforms as its core assets. As an independent company, Vantive needs to demonstrate an innovation pipeline to institutional investors — it cannot rely on Baxter's broader portfolio to tell a growth story. This creates direct structural incentive for Vantive to establish co-development or corporate venture relationships with early-stage kidney care device companies.

The ask to Vantive is concrete: access to PrisMax circuit specifications and one affiliated clinical site for ex-vivo validation, in exchange for a right-of-first-negotiation on commercial integration. This is low-cost for Vantive and positions them as the preferred acquirer before FDA clearance is even filed.

Strategic Timing — When Does the Acquisition Window Open?

Based on the Hema Metrics, Cheetah Medical, and Masimo precedents, the acquisition discussion begins when:

  1. FDA clearance is received or imminent (removes regulatory risk for the acquirer)
  2. Clinical accuracy data is published in a peer-reviewed journal (proves the sensor works as claimed)
  3. A credible clinical utility signal exists — either from pilot data or from investigator enthusiasm at key academic centres

At this stage, the technology has de-risked sufficiently that Baxter or Fresenius corporate development teams will engage. The conversation typically starts as a licensing discussion ("Can we integrate this into PrisMax as an accessory?") and evolves into an acquisition offer if the IP position is strong and the clinical signal is credible.

Timeline estimate: Year 4–5 from programme start is when the acquisition window opens. This assumes: Year 1–2 bench + ex-vivo validation, Year 2–3 FIH observational study + 510(k) submission, Year 3–4 clearance + clinical publication, Year 4–5 acquisition negotiation or follow-on clinical utility study to strengthen valuation.

Section 4

Product Requirements — What Baxter and Fresenius Will Accept

The device must be designed from day one to integrate with the PrisMax/Prismaflex and multiFiltrate platforms. If it requires circuit redesign or machine firmware changes to function, it will not be acquired — it will be ignored or copied.

Non-Negotiable Integration Requirements

1. Uses Existing Circuit Sampling Ports

The Baxter PrisMax and Prismaflex machines have standardised blood sampling ports: pre-filter arterial line port, post-filter venous line port, and effluent port. The sensor must connect to these ports using standard Luer-lock or proprietary Baxter/Fresenius connectors — no custom circuit modifications.

Why This Matters
If the device requires Baxter to redesign the PrisMax bloodline set or add a new sampling port, it becomes a machine engineering project, not an accessory integration. Machine redesigns take 3–5 years and trigger full regulatory resubmission. Accessories that use existing ports can be integrated in 6–12 months.

2. Disposable Sensor Element + Reusable Electronics

The business model must match Baxter and Fresenius's consumables-driven revenue model. This means:

  • The sensing element (microfluidic chip, ISE membrane, plasma separation cartridge) is a single-use disposable sold per CRRT circuit (every 72 hours)
  • The readout electronics, calibration module, and display are reusable capital equipment that sits on the CRRT machine or IV pole
  • Pricing: disposable priced at $150–250 per circuit (vs $800–1200 for the hemofilter itself); reusable electronics priced at $8K–12K per ICU bed

3. Meets Blood-Contacting Device Biocompatibility Standards

ISO 10993 biocompatibility testing is mandatory. The device must pass:

  • Cytotoxicity, sensitization, and irritation testing (10993-5, -10)
  • Haemocompatibility testing (10993-4): hemolysis, platelet activation, coagulation
  • Thrombogenicity evaluation if the device has >2 mL priming volume or creates stagnant flow zones

4. Does Not Disrupt Circuit Alarms or Flow Dynamics

CRRT machines monitor blood flow rate (100–200 mL/min typically), access pressure (−100 to −200 mmHg), return pressure (+100 to +300 mmHg), and transmembrane pressure (TMP). If the sensor causes pressure drops that trigger machine alarms or reduce filter efficiency, it will not be clinically acceptable.

Design constraint: any sidestream extraction for plasma separation or calibration must be ≤5 mL/min (2.5–5% of circuit flow) and must return fluid to the circuit to avoid net volume loss.

5. Provides Continuous Data Stream to CRRT Machine

The sensor must output pfCa²⁺ readings in real-time (≥1 reading per minute) via a standard protocol (RS-232, Modbus, or Ethernet) so that Baxter or Fresenius can display the data on the PrisMax/multiFiltrate touchscreen and log it for later analysis. Stand-alone display-only devices are less attractive because they do not integrate into the machine's existing alarm and trending infrastructure.

Section 5

Regulatory Strategy — FDA 510(k) as the Acquisition Gateway

The regulatory pathway is FDA 510(k) clearance (USA) + CE Mark under IVDR (EU). Both are required because:

  • The US is the primary market for CRRT (largest installed base, highest per-patient spending)
  • EU regulatory clearance significantly increases acquisition valuation because it de-risks European commercialisation for the acquirer

510(k) Pathway — Device Classification and Predicate Strategy

Device Classification

The pfCa²⁺ sensor will be classified as:

  • Product code: BSO — "System, Test, Blood Gases, And pH"
  • Class II (Special Controls)
  • Regulation: 21 CFR 862.1345 — Blood Gas Analyzer
  • Review panel: Clinical Chemistry

This classification allows the 510(k) pathway if substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate device can be demonstrated.

Predicate Device Strategy

The strongest predicate is:

  • Radiometer ABL90 FLEX (K141086, cleared 2014) — a point-of-care blood gas analyser with iCa²⁺ measurement capability
  • Rationale for substantial equivalence: same intended use (iCa²⁺ measurement in critical care patients), same measurement principle (ion-selective electrode potentiometry), same sample type (whole blood or plasma), same performance specifications (accuracy ±0.03 mmol/L vs reference method)

Key difference to address in 510(k): the predicate is a benchtop analyser with batch sampling; the proposed device is an inline continuous monitor. This is a technological difference but not a fundamental change in intended use or measurement principle, so it should not trigger a De Novo requirement. However, FDA may request additional performance data demonstrating that continuous inline measurement does not introduce new risks (e.g., circuit clotting, air embolism, infection) compared to batch sampling.

Performance Testing Requirements for 510(k) Submission

The 510(k) submission will require:

  1. Analytical performance studies
    • Accuracy vs reference method (arterial blood gas analyser): MARD ≤5% across 0.10–1.50 mmol/L range, with specific focus on 0.25–0.50 mmol/L (post-filter RCA range)
    • Precision: CV ≤3% at three concentration levels (0.25, 0.75, 1.25 mmol/L)
    • Linearity: r² ≥0.98 over measurement range
    • Interference testing: hematocrit (20–60%), citrate (up to 5 mmol/L), pH (7.1–7.6), hemolysis, lipemia
  2. Biocompatibility testing
    • ISO 10993-5 (cytotoxicity), -10 (sensitization), -4 (haemocompatibility)
    • Conducted at ISO 17025 accredited lab (e.g., Nelson Labs, WuXi AppTec)
  3. Electrical safety and EMC testing
    • IEC 60601-1 (medical electrical equipment safety)
    • IEC 60601-1-2 (electromagnetic compatibility)
  4. Clinical performance study
    • n=50–100 ICU patients on CRRT with RCA
    • Blinded observational design: sensor readings are collected but not used for clinical decisions
    • Primary endpoint: agreement with ABG reference (Bland-Altman analysis, MARD)
    • Secondary endpoints: nursing workflow time, adverse events (circuit clotting, sensor malfunction)
    • Conducted under FDA IDE (Investigational Device Exemption) at 1–2 sites
  5. Sterilisation validation
    • If using ethylene oxide (EtO): ISO 11135 validation at contract steriliser
    • If using gamma irradiation: ISO 11137 validation
  6. Shelf life and stability testing
    • Real-time stability: 24 months at accelerated conditions (40°C / 75% RH) to support 2-year shelf life claim
    • Functional testing of disposable sensor after storage

CE Mark Strategy (EU IVDR)

Under the EU In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR 2017/746), the device will be classified as Class C (medium-high risk IVD for patient management decisions). This requires:

  • Full quality management system (ISO 13485 certification)
  • Notified Body review and approval
  • Clinical performance study conducted in EU or acceptance of US clinical data under reciprocal agreement

Notified Body selection: BSI (UK), TÜV SÜD (Germany), or DEKRA (Netherlands) are the primary choices for IVD IVDR certification. Timeline: 18–24 months from dossier submission to certificate issuance.

Strategic note: EU regulatory clearance can be pursued in parallel with FDA 510(k) but is lower priority for the acquisition thesis. Baxter and Fresenius will value FDA clearance more highly because the US market drives >60% of global CRRT revenue.

Section 6

Clinical Validation Strategy

Phase 1: Accuracy Study (Regulatory Requirement)

Objective: Demonstrate that the sensor accurately measures post-filter iCa²⁺ in the RCA-CRRT range (0.25–0.50 mmol/L) compared to ABG reference.

Study design:

  • Observational, blinded (sensor readings not used for clinical decisions)
  • n=50–100 ICU patients on CRRT with regional citrate anticoagulation
  • Setting: 1–2 academic medical centres with high CRRT volume (e.g., UCSF, Cleveland Clinic, Apollo Chennai, AIIMS Delhi)
  • Primary endpoint: Mean Absolute Relative Difference (MARD) vs ABG reference; target MARD <10%
  • Secondary endpoints: nursing workflow time savings, sensor drift over 72h, adverse events

Rationale for sample size: n=50 is sufficient for FDA 510(k) performance validation if the device shows consistent accuracy. n=100 provides stronger statistical power for subgroup analysis (e.g., performance at very low Ca²⁺ <0.30 mmol/L, performance in septic vs non-septic patients).

Phase 2: Clinical Utility Study (Acquisition Value Driver)

Objective: Demonstrate that continuous pfCa²⁺ monitoring improves clinical outcomes or workflow efficiency.

Study design options:

  1. Filter lifespan endpoint (faster, lower cost)
    • Hypothesis: continuous monitoring allows tighter citrate dose titration → longer filter lifespan
    • Primary endpoint: median filter lifespan (hours) in intervention group (continuous monitoring) vs control (standard 4-hourly ABG)
    • Target improvement: ≥6 hours increase in median filter life (e.g., 48h → 54h)
    • n=100–150 patients (50–75 per arm)
  2. Nursing time reduction endpoint
    • Hypothesis: continuous monitoring reduces frequency of manual ABG sampling → nursing time savings
    • Primary endpoint: nursing time spent on CRRT monitoring (time-motion study)
    • Target reduction: ≥20 minutes per patient per shift
    • n=50–100 patients
  3. Citrate safety endpoint (highest clinical impact, highest cost)
    • Hypothesis: continuous monitoring reduces incidence of citrate accumulation or hypocalcemia
    • Primary endpoint: incidence of citrate toxicity (total Ca / ionised Ca ratio >2.5) or systemic hypocalcemia
    • This requires a multicentre RCT with n=300–500 patients and will take 2–3 years to complete

Recommended approach: Start with Option 1 (filter lifespan) as the first clinical utility signal post-510(k) clearance. This can be conducted as a single-centre prospective cohort study with historical controls. If the signal is positive, escalate to Option 3 (safety endpoint RCT) as part of the post-acquisition product development roadmap led by Baxter or Fresenius.

Section 7

Competitive Landscape Analysis

The competitive landscape consists of three layers: patent holders, active companies, and technological approaches. Understanding where freedom-to-operate exists and where direct collision will occur is critical for positioning this technology for acquisition.

Key Finding: No commercial continuous inline blood chemistry sensor exists for CRRT or ECMO. Stage 3 and 4 development are empty. The combined-panel continuous inline sensing space remains entirely open for a first mover. However, Kalium Health holds the strongest IP position for electrolyte-only sensing in dialysis circuits.

Strategic Positioning — Five Key Conclusions

01 — The Electrolyte-Only Lane Is Being Entered by Kalium Health

Kalium Health has active patents on ISE-based inline electrolyte sensing specifically in extracorporeal circuits and has begun clinical validation. If competing on electrolytes alone, this effort would be 3–4 years behind a well-funded competitor.

Differentiating move: Add blood gas measurement (pH, pCO₂, pO₂) in the same disposable module. Kalium has no gas-sensing IP and is not targeting ECMO. This creates a combined panel that is 10× more valuable clinically, especially in ECMO+CRRT patients (who represent ~30–40% of ECMO patients).

02 — Baxter/Gambro Owns the Circuit Access

The Prismaflex/PrisMax circuit has standardised blood-sampling ports (pre-filter, post-filter, effluent). The device should be designed as a clip-on module that uses these existing ports rather than requiring circuit modification.

Strategic implication: This makes adoption non-disruptive for Baxter and avoids negotiating a proprietary connector. Baxter's citrate monitoring patent (WO2020120004A1) creates an IP risk for automated Ca²⁺-based citrate safety monitoring claims — consider designing around their monitoring logic rather than competing on the claim.

03 — Freedom-to-Operate Requires Clearance Around Two Granted Patents

Kalium Health's GB2614308A (granted Oct 2024) covers an analyte sensor unit fluidically coupled to the extracorporeal blood circuit with an auxiliary calibration circuit. Fresenius's US20200030515A1 (granted Nov 2022) covers potassium sensing with microfluidic chip embodiment.

Action required: Secure FTO opinion before prototype filing. Differentiating claim space: (1) combined electrolyte + gas sensing in single module, (2) membrane-separated diffusion architecture (blood never touches sensor), (3) ECMO-specific circuit integration with gas exchange calculation.

04 — The Clinical Validation Gap Is a Genuine Opportunity

ClinicalTrials.gov search found zero trials directly validating inline continuous blood chemistry monitoring in CRRT or ECMO circuits. The LIBERATE trial (NCT04218370, UCSF) showed the clinical need for real-time kidney recovery monitoring.

Partnership opportunity: Partner with LIBERATE investigators to run a concurrent observational accuracy sub-study at low incremental cost. This creates a high-impact publication, clinical KOL relationships, and regulatory data simultaneously.

05 — What Has Not Worked: Lessons from Terminated Approaches

TEG-based POC management in ECMO (NCT02887820) terminated at n=15 of 40. Medtronic's long-term ISE (EP1797417A2, expired 2008) never reached commercialisation. Samsung's disc-based microfluidic electrolyte device (2010) is expired and abandoned.

Key lessons: Enrolment in critically ill ECMO patients is extremely slow; design trials with shorter follow-up. ISE drift and biocompatibility at multi-day use remain core unsolved problems. The market is waiting for continuous monitoring, not improved batch testing.
